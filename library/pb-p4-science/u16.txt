Unit 17: Physical changes and chemical changes

In this unit you will learn to:
- identify changes where no new substance is made (physical changes)
- identify changes where a new substance is made (chemical changes)
- give everyday examples of each

Let's start
You melt chocolate, then let it cool: it is still chocolate. You burn a piece of paper: you cannot turn the ash back into paper. What is the difference?

Physical changes: no new substance
In a physical change, the shape, size or state of something changes, but no new substance is made. Many physical changes can be reversed.
- Dissolving: sugar dissolves in water. It is still sugar: if the water evaporates, the sugar is left behind.
- Evaporation: wet clothes dry in the sun. The water turns into water vapour.
- Melting and freezing: ice melts into water; water freezes into ice.
- Squeezing: squashing a piece of plasticine or a sponge.
- Stretching: pulling a rubber band.
- Cutting, folding, tearing or crushing: the pieces are still the same material.

Chemical changes: a new substance is made
In a chemical change, a new substance is made. Most chemical changes cannot be reversed.
- Rusting: iron reacts with water and air to make reddish-brown rust. Rust is weak and flaky.
- Burning: wood or paper burns to make ash and smoke. Burning gives out heat and light.
- Food rotting: bread grows mould and fruit goes bad. It smells and tastes different.
- Cooking: an egg turns from runny to solid when fried; bread turns brown when toasted.
- Baking: cake mixture turns into a soft, spongy cake.
- Fireworks exploding.

Signs that a chemical change may have happened
- a change of colour (an apple slice turning brown, iron rusting)
- bubbles of gas
- a new smell
- heat or light given out
- it cannot easily be changed back

Physical or chemical?
- Ice cream melting → physical (it can freeze again; still ice cream).
- A candle: the wax melting is physical; the wick and wax burning is chemical.
- Salt dissolving in water → physical.
- A bike chain going rusty → chemical.

Preventing rust
Rust needs water and air. We can stop iron rusting by painting it, oiling it, covering it with plastic or another metal, or keeping it dry.

Common mix-ups
- Thinking dissolving makes the sugar disappear. It is still there: you can taste it.
- Thinking melting is a chemical change because it looks different. No new substance is made.
- Thinking all changes that cannot be reversed are chemical. Cutting your hair cannot be reversed, but it is a physical change.

Challenge (for fast learners)
- Why are iron gates in Hong Kong painted, especially near the sea? (Paint keeps water and air away; salty sea air makes iron rust faster.)

Try this at home
Put a steel nail (with no coating) in a cup with a little water and leave another nail in a dry, closed jar. Check them after a week.

Key words
- physical change: a change where no new substance is made (物理變化)
- chemical change: a change where a new substance is made (化學變化)
- substance: a kind of matter (物質)
- dissolve: mix completely into a liquid (溶解)
- evaporate: change from a liquid into a gas (蒸發)
- rust: a reddish-brown substance formed on iron (鐵鏽)
- reverse: change back to how it was (還原)
